# Post-crash EV assessment sold to insurers (31 Oct 2026)

Multi-agent research (5 angles, a skeptic per angle, one synthesis; run wf_26829a94-735). Full findings and skeptic notes: `data/post_crash_raw.json`. Not yet reviewed line by line by a human; marketing claims and single-source numbers are flagged in the text.

## Verdict
VERDICT (31 Oct 2026): Insurers will probably not pay for a standalone "post-crash battery scan" in Latvia today. They might pay for a faster, defensible decision on a small number of expensive EV claims. Right now the evidence supports a pilot hypothesis, not a proven product.

What insurers would be paying for, in order of how well the evidence supports it:
1. Shorter or avoided tow, battery-removal and quarantine cost. Allianz says EV towing cost about double that of conventional cars in 2025, with a top claim of EUR 19,217 (trade press, auto-motor-und-sport, 4 Mar 2026). VDA says tow firms often remove HV batteries from fear of fire even after minor damage (AUTOHAUS, 14 Jan 2026). This is the best-documented cost pool, but it is shrinking. VW and BMW cut initial quarantine to 24 h with temperature monitoring (AZT, firmenauto, 10 Aug 2026). A German court capped daily storage rates (LG Koblenz, a plug-in hybrid, first instance, May 2026).
2. Avoiding a premature write-off or full pack swap when only the casing or bracket is hit. Thatcham's EV Blueprint (Mar 2026) gives the example of a 3-year-old EV written off after minor bracket damage. It quantifies no savings. AZT found 3 of about 8 battery cases in its sample were solved with a casing swap of EUR 1,100-2,200 instead of a EUR 13,300-43,300 pack.
3. Evidence they can show a regulator, a claimant or a court. This is the weakest point. A third-party scan cannot override OEM repair rules. AZT says some OEMs mandate replacement after airbag or pyrotechnic deployment, and an insurer owes restoration of the original condition.

Why not yet:
- Volume is tiny. AZT found the battery involved in about 2% of EV collision claims (n=400, about 8 cases, reportedly 2021 claims) but about 7% of claim cost. Latvia has about 13,000-13,900 EVs. My own estimate is about 57 triggered assessments per year market-wide (see value estimate). That is not a business by itself.
- Nobody pays for this yet. Across all four research angles I found no insurer pilot, tender or case study with measured savings from post-crash battery assessment, and no vendor selling one at a published price. This is either an open gap or a signal of weak demand, and we cannot tell which from desk research.
- Detection is limited. See the detect/cannot-detect section.
- OEM-independent data is the insurers' actual complaint (Allianz, Thatcham, GDV). The likely winners are OEMs (VW's assessment catalogue for MEB packs), standards bodies (Thatcham/BSI, SVV, TUV-Verband, VDA) and the incumbent experts (DEKRA, TUV, AZT), not a start-up.

What would convince an insurer:
(a) a retrospective review of their own EV claim files showing where a scan would have changed a decision;
(b) the scan agreeing with the OEM dealer diagnostic in parallel testing;
(c) zero false "all clear" verdicts on packs that later fail;
(d) turnaround within 24 h of the tow;
(e) liability cover for a "safe to store or repair" verdict;
(f) a price below the value of one avoided day of quarantine plus a small share of an avoided pack swap.

Recommended framing for the pitch: do not sell "a scan". Sell a post-crash decision and handling service for damaged EV packs. It has three parts: triage to the SVV-style criteria, safe storage and routing, then repair, second life or recycling through our chain. The scan is the entry gate. Revenue comes from handling and routing, not from the scan fee. Present all savings as hypotheses to be tested in a pilot.

Date corrections for the team brief: the SVV annex is signed 31 Oct 2025 (v9) and was posted in March 2026. The brief says Feb 2026. The 10 mm casing figure is an exclusion-and-disposal trigger, not an "escalation" trigger.

## How post-crash assessment works today
- Stage 1, scene and tow: police or rescue services secure the car. A tow firm, often without a standard rule, decides on transport (with or without a fire-protection container) and whether the pack is removed. The Swiss SVV/LiBaService24 annex (v9, signed 31 Oct 2025, posted Mar 2026, [official but voluntary guideline]) calls this the hazard analysis, with HV1 plus HV2 training as the minimum. URL: https://svv.ch/sites/default/files/media/documents/2026-03/202600227_Anhang%20Leitfaden_HV-Batterie%20Analyse_DE.pdf
- Stage 2, workshop function analysis on a lift (SVV annex): review documents (police report, event type 4-8); check physical impact (rollover, shifted mounting points, intruding parts); visual check of underbody and casing (dents, cracks, holes, cooling plates and leaks, water ingress, heat above 60 C, soot, extinguishing agent, airbag and belt-pretensioner deployment, connectors and bolts); electrical read-out (fault memory, SoC, cell and module voltages and temperatures, cell voltage difference, insulation values, SoH, pyrofuse status). Live HV work needs HV3. Outcome is one of four classes: functional, repairable, damaged/defective, significantly damaged/critical. The test is non-destructive.
- SVV exclusion rules (no further testing, dispose): casing deformation 10 mm or more deep, torn casing or holes, sustained casing heat above 60 C, multiple rollover, significant shift at mounting points, foreign object penetrating the pack, cells involved in fire, water in the pack. The guide states its thresholds are averages from OEM data and literature, and that OEM instructions always prevail. Strictly, only 'event 8' and water-in-pack sit in the 'must be disposed' table. The others sit in the 'damaged/defective' table, though the intro text still says excluded packs are disposed of without closer testing (skeptic check of the PDF text).
- Germany: a VDA-led industry guideline with ZDK (Jan 2026, free) covers transport, interim storage and quarantine. The TUV-Verband whitepaper (reported 6-11 May 2026 and 7 Sep 2026) names insulation resistance, thermography, vehicle data and fault logs, and a cost-benefit test against replacement value. I found no numeric thresholds in the press coverage and could not retrieve the full documents. [trade press summaries]
- Insurer or expert decision: a claims handler or expert compares repair cost with vehicle value. OEM rules decide whether casing repair or module exchange is allowed. Many OEMs allow only whole-pack or casing replacement (AZT position paper 14 Dec 2023). Allianz has seen scratched packs with probably undamaged cells that were written off for lack of OEM diagnostic data (Reuters via devdiscourse, 20 Mar 2023).
- Quarantine practice has no harmonised standard. UK: 15 m separation for damaged EVs awaiting repair (Thatcham/Innovate UK coverage, 2023). US: 50 ft (NTSB, collisionweek 14 Jan 2021). Swiss AGVS: critical packs outdoors, observed, in a safety container (4 Mar 2026). Germany: the fire-association observation period is reported as 72 h in one place and at least 24 h in another, both secondary. VW and BMW use 24 h plus temperature monitoring (AZT, 10 Aug 2026). A Swedish fire officer cited two weeks and 10 m (SVT, practitioner statement, not a rule).
- Duration and price of the checks: not found as an official or vendor price. One AI-assisted German blog (elektronik-zeit.de, 11 Oct 2026) says EUR 150-300 and 1-2 h for a 'Lite' diagnosis, and 2-3 working days for a 'Full' one. The skeptic flagged it as unsourced, so do not use it as a price anchor. [low confidence]
- AZT says manufacturer tools usually tell reliably whether a battery is faulty. The hard parts are the on-site assessment, where to park safely, and the fact that independent workshops often lack the diagnostic scope (firmenauto.de, 10 Aug 2026, trade press). So the gap is OEM-independent access and the scene and storage decision, not the absence of any diagnostic.
- Latvia: I found no published Latvian procedure, insurer statement or VUGD guideline for assessing or quarantining crashed EV batteries. ERGO's CASCO terms define total loss only as repair that is technically impossible or economically unjustified. LTAB certifies OCTA experts and keeps the loss-assessment methodology. [searched, not found]

## What an assessment can and cannot detect
- CAN detect reliably: gross mechanical damage (casing dents measured in mm, cracks, holes, shifted mounts); water ingress; fire or heat exposure; open BMS fault codes; insulation faults; large cell-voltage or temperature deviations that the BMS reports; pyrofuse and airbag status; a fast-moving temperature rise during an observation period. Sources: SVV annex 2025/26 [official]; TUV-Verband, AUTOHAUS and ecomento May and Sep 2026 [trade press].
- CAN detect with caveats: SoH and capacity (AVILOO, DEKRA 15-minute test, TWAICE) measure capacity, not mechanical integrity. They do not show a cracked cell housing or a latent separator defect. Thermography and X-ray show geometry and heat, not electrochemical state.
- CANNOT reliably prove an apparently intact pack is safe. SVV itself says a pack can carry thermal, mechanical, electrical or chemical stress that is possibly not visible from outside, and that it then has an 'undefined state'. TUV SUD says an unremarkable exterior does not rule out damage. TUV-Verband says internal short circuits and thermal instability can develop with a delay. [official / trade press]
- CANNOT rule out delayed events with a one-time check. NHTSA's 2011 Chevrolet Volt test ended in a fire 21 days after the crash test (NHTSA document INOA-PE11037). The skeptic notes this was a worst-case lab protocol with post-crash rotation and coolant loss, on one vehicle, so it shows possibility, not frequency. I found no source for the share of crashed EVs that suffer delayed thermal runaway. [not found]
- Fraunhofer AIR X-ray (about 10 min per vehicle, more than 100 cars in the 2024 pilot, 2026 software that flags displaced or swollen cells) is research-stage. It has no published price and no insurer customer. In the ID.3 lab crash case the pack was rated EUCAR 0, yet X-ray alone did not justify reuse. DEKRA hazard evaluation, EDR and BMS data, and electrical tests were also needed (electrive, 22 Sep 2026). Fraunhofer says it does not replace electrical tests.
- CANNOT change an outcome that OEM rules fix. If the OEM mandates pack replacement after airbag deployment or the pack is structural (the Tesla Model Y 'zero repairability' remark attributed to Sandy Munro, secondary), a third-party scan has no decision value. Insurer savings depend on OEM or liability-body acceptance, not on detection accuracy (AZT 14 Dec 2023: crash load cannot be derived from airbag triggering).
- CANNOT see inside the pack with drive-through camera scanners (UVeye type). They see the exterior and underside only.
- Known blind spots of simple diagnostics, from the low-confidence German blog (11 Oct 2026): micro-deformation of the tray with no visible marks, detached module connectors or internal sensor cables, insulation damage that only shows in humidity, and cooling-loop damage that shows at the next fast-charge. A clean generic OBD2 scan does not confirm HV health. Treat as plausible but unverified.
- Net for the pitch: the defensible claim is 'faster, documented triage that finds gross damage and flags uncertain packs for quarantine or disposal'. It is not 'we detect battery damage' and not 'we certify a pack as safe'.

## Where insurers lose money on EVs
- **Full battery replacement in the tail of underbody and impact claims** — AZT/Allianz sample of 400 EV Vollkasko collision claims (reportedly 2021 claims, published Jan 2023): HV battery involved in 2% of claims (about 8 cases) but about 7% of claim cost. Five cases needed a new pack at EUR 13,300-43,300; three more were saved with a casing swap at EUR 1,100-2,200. The n=8 means very wide uncertainty and the prices are 2021. URL: https://www.azt-automotive.com/_Resources/Persistent/b08ac2e8d9685432c0b8c7ded36a0f886053b399/AZT%20Analyse%20Kollisionssch%C3%A4den%20HV%20Fzge.pdf [official, insurer research]. Newer range quoted by AZT: battery damage from under EUR 1,000 to EUR 29,000 (firmenauto.de, 10 Aug 2026, trade press). Allianz press release 17 Oct 2023: pack replacement EUR 13,000 to about EUR 46,000 [official]. *Could an assessment reduce it?* Partly. Only where the OEM permits casing or module repair, or where an independent verdict is accepted. The gain is in the share of cases where the pack is fine but would be swapped. That share is unknown (own assumption in the value estimate).
- **Write-off or pack swap triggered by minor casing or bracket damage or by airbag deployment under OEM rules** — Thatcham EV Blueprint (4-5 Mar 2026): mandated procedures frequently produce total losses; example is a 3-year-old EV written off after minor side impact on a battery bracket; battery up to 40% of vehicle value; no quantified savings published (insurance-edge.net 4 Mar 2026; repairerdrivennews.com 5 Mar 2026) [trade press relaying Thatcham]. Swedbank Estonia: when airbag deployment forces pack replacement, total claims were over EUR 50,000 and some reached EUR 85,000 (ERR, 14 Feb 2024; single insurer, tail examples). AZT 14 Dec 2023: some OEMs require replacement after pyrotechnic deployment. *Could an assessment reduce it?* Only if OEM rules allow deviation. Thatcham's own recommendation is for standards and repairable designs, not for a start-up scan. High value in theory, low controllability.
- **Towing, battery removal and special transport** — Allianz: 2025 EV towing costs about double those of conventional vehicles; highest single Allianz claim EUR 19,217. A Spiegel case (Peugeot e-208): towing invoice over EUR 20,000, including EUR 2,450 net for battery removal and storage box and EUR 1,650 for a battery expert (auto-motor-und-sport.de, 4 Mar 2026) [trade press]. The doubling is a ratio, and I found no absolute average cost per EV tow. *Could an assessment reduce it?* Yes, if a fast on-scene or early triage prevents unnecessary pack removal and container transport. This is the best-supported pool, but the cases cited are outliers and the practice is already being corrected by the VDA/GDV/ADAC guideline (Dec 2025 / Jan 2026).
- **Quarantine and storage** — Thatcham 2023: a 48 h quarantine adds at least GBP 60 per claim and a yard for 100 ICE cars holds about 2 quarantined EVs (insurance-edge.net 5 Jul 2023, trade press). Harz case: storage fee about EUR 1,300 plus more than EUR 100 per further day (firmenauto.de, 21 Jan 2026). LG Koblenz (Az. 14 O 169/24, 4 May 2026, a plug-in hybrid, first instance): a tow firm claimed EUR 38,569.81 including EUR 95/day quarantine; the court awarded EUR 7,596.96 in total storage at EUR 76.16/day for 5 days then EUR 19.04/day. The EUR 7,596.96 is the total, not an extra amount (LTO 4 Jun 2026; DATEV Magazin 16 Jun 2026) [legal press]. *Could an assessment reduce it?* Yes, by cutting quarantine days. The pool is shrinking (OEM 24 h rule, court caps), so treat it as modest per claim.
- **Higher general EV claim severity (not battery-specific)** — GDV (Aug 2025, 53 EV model-series pairs): Vollkasko severity about +15-20% vs comparable combustion cars, frequency about -10-15% (autohaus.de 18 Aug 2025, trade press). Thatcham 2023: BEV claims about +25.5% and 14% slower to repair. Mitchell Q1 2026 (US): repairable severity BEV USD 6,042 vs ICE USD 4,902 [vendor / trade press]. Repair-cost gap is converging (CCC: EV vs hybrid gap USD 1,809 in 2020 to USD 445 in 2025 for 7+ year old vehicles). *Could an assessment reduce it?* Mostly no. This is parts and labour, not battery assessment. It shows EVs cost more, but the cost is spread across many claims.
- **Total-loss rate** — EVs are NOT written off more often overall. Mitchell Q1 2026: BEV 12% vs new ICE 13% (repairerdrivennews.com, 21 May 2026). Cap HPI UK (data 2015 to Aug 2024): under-5-year-old EVs 0.9% vs 1.89% petrol/diesel [vendor]. Do NOT use CCC's 12.7% vs 23.1%: the 23.1% is an all-age, undefined-denominator industry rate. Opposing estimate: FWD Consulting (Dec 2025) says about 1 in 5 collided EVs are written off, methodology not verified. Drivers: falling used-EV values (CCC: EV values -46% since Oct 2022) as much as battery damage. Mitchell notes that once the battery is damaged, total-loss likelihood rises significantly. *Could an assessment reduce it?* Only for young, high-value EVs where repair is feasible. For older, cheap EVs, repair exceeds value either way.
- **Fire** — Sweden MSB: 40 fires in electric/hybrid passenger cars in 2024 (38 in 2023), verified only via Vi Bilagare. GDV: about 13,600 insured car fires in 2024 with about EUR 100m paid, all drivetrains, and no evidence EVs burn more. Latvia VUGD: 523 vehicle fires in 2025 (397 passenger cars), with no EV split; LAA chief says about 90% of burned cars are older than 5 years and about 90% diesel [official / trade press]. Frequency of delayed post-crash re-ignition and cost per EV fire: not found. Allianz UK average lithium-battery fire claim GBP 50,000 covers all lithium types, not EV cars. *Could an assessment reduce it?* Narrative value is high, expected-loss value is not supported by frequency data. Do not lead the pitch with fire.
- **Brand-workshop labour and diagnostic scope** — AZT: brand workshops charge almost 50% higher hourly rates; independent workshops often lack the diagnostic scope (firmenauto.de, 10 Aug 2026). Estonia: labour about EUR 70/h (up to 90) vs EUR 160-190 at Finnish Tesla service (ERR 14 Feb 2024). *Could an assessment reduce it?* Possibly, by enabling independent repair decisions. Unquantified.
- **Salvage and residual value of damaged EVs and packs** — No usable figure found for damaged-EV auction price as a share of pre-loss value (Copart, IAA, BCA, Autoplius, Norwegian and Swedish salvage all searched). Synetiq (UK salvage, company estimate) says at least 95% of cells in stored packs are undamaged, but that is a salvage firm's estimate for packs in its yard, not crash-scratched packs (Reuters via devdiscourse, 20 Mar 2023). Allianz 2023: capacity loss of up to 20% can cut resale value by up to 25%. *Could an assessment reduce it?* Plausibly (a scan certificate supports a buyer's confidence), but no data show the uplift. Treat as an assumption to test.

## Competitors and alternatives
- **Tractable**: AI photo damage estimating and total-loss triage for insurers (Geico, Aviva, Hartford, Japanese insurers named by vendor and trade press). No documented HV-battery capability. Price/model: Not public. Funding: USD 65M Series E led by SoftBank Vision Fund 2 (CollisionWeek 18 Jul 2023). Savings claims (for example 360,000 adjuster hours at a Japanese insurer) are vendor-stated. Threat or partner: Low direct threat (exterior only). Possible partner or data source for triage routing. A battery module added later would be the threat.
- **CCC Intelligent Solutions**: US claims platform with AI total-loss routing; claims more than 90% accuracy (vendor). No battery-health assessment found. Price/model: Platform software; AI products about 10% of revenue per a third-party analysis of Q1 2026. US focus. Threat or partner: Low for Latvia. Shows insurers buy claims-workflow software, and sets the bar for integration.
- **Solera / Audatex (Qapter)**: Estimating platform used by insurers and repairers, with photo AI. Only EV item found is an estimation training course (Oct 2025). Audatex Baltics UAB exists in Vilnius. Price/model: Platform; no public price. Threat or partner: Medium as a channel. If we win a pilot, the output must integrate with or accompany the Audatex-style estimate.
- **Ravin AI**: Mobile and fixed-camera vehicle inspection for insurers and fleets. Exterior, interior, tyres and glass; no battery health. Price/model: AWS Marketplace enterprise listing USD 84,000 per 12 months for up to 20,000 inspections (about USD 4.20 per inspection, own arithmetic at full utilisation). Backers include IAG's Firemark Ventures and KAR Global (USD 15M Series A, 2023). Threat or partner: Low. Useful only as evidence of subscription pricing to insurers. Not a valid anchor for a battery assessment.
- **UVeye**: Drive-through camera scanning of exterior and underbody. W.R. Berkley (insurer) is a repeat investor. Cannot see battery state of health. Price/model: Per-scan price and any claims programme: not found. Threat or partner: Medium. It could add casing-deformation screening cheaply. A partner for the gross-damage step, not a competitor on SoH.
- **AVILOO**: Austrian SoH test (about 3 minutes, compatible with over 96% of EVs) for the used-car trade. EUR 30M investment (Feb 2026). Battery warranty in 24 European markets, not Latvia, Estonia or Lithuania (renewableenergymagazine 7 Sep 2026). Price/model: Consumer test EUR 99 (ecomento); OeAMTC Flash test EUR 69. Customers are dealers, leasing, auctions and fleets. No insurer named as a post-crash customer. Threat or partner: Medium threat if it extends to crash cases; most likely a partner or supplier for SoH. It measures capacity, not crash damage.
- **TWAICE**: Cloud battery analytics for OEMs, fleets and storage. Munich Re's Great Lakes backs an accuracy guarantee that pays 8 times the analytics price if results fall outside the guaranteed accuracy (electrive 12 Dec 2022). It needs a telematics data stream. Price/model: Enterprise analytics. Funding includes USD 30M Series B extension (2022) and EUR 24M from the EIB (Preqin, Feb 2026, aggregator). Threat or partner: Closest precedent for insurer-backed battery analytics, but for storage and fleets, not post-crash. A model for how an insurer-backed accuracy guarantee could work for us.
- **Volytica and Generational**: Volytica (Dresden, Fraunhofer spin-off): charger-based tests for fleets. Generational (UK): 8,000+ assessments, average SoH 95.15%, distribution to about 6,000 UK workshops via Maverick Diagnostics. Price/model: Not public; no insurer deal found for either. Threat or partner: Low. Possible tool suppliers for the SoH step.
- **Fraunhofer IIS AIR (X-ray)**: Drive-through X-ray of whole vehicles, about 10 min per vehicle, with auto-detection of displaced or swollen cells (electrive 22 Sep 2026). Research or pilot stage with DEKRA and Munich UAS. Price/model: No price, no insurer customer, no deployment announced. Threat or partner: Long-term technology threat and potential partner. Not a Baltic option today.
- **DEKRA, TUV SUD, TUV-Verband, AZT, Thatcham**: Experts and standard setters. DEKRA offers a 15-minute capacity test and damage expertise for insurers; TUV SUD offers SoH diagnosis; TUV-Verband published an assessment whitepaper (Sep 2026); AZT and Thatcham define what insurers ask for. Thatcham's Blueprint asks for open, standardised HV diagnostics on common equipment and warns against specialised subscription tools for battery removal and reinstatement. Price/model: No per-assessment price found for any of them. Threat or partner: High in Germany and the UK, where they set methods and are trusted. Low presence in Latvia. Likely partners for method credibility and potential white-label channel. Their guidance also pressures any per-assessment price upward only if we can prove value.
- **OEM assessment catalogues and dealer diagnostics (for example VW's MEB battery assessment catalogue)**: OEMs issue their own post-crash criteria to dealers, workshops, experts and insurers. AUTOHAUS (16 Jan 2023) reports AZT's Lauterwasser saying the VW catalogue can save about EUR 10,000-30,000 per case by avoiding full HV unit replacement. [single trade-press quote, 2023]. Price/model: Bundled into dealer service; subscription portals. Threat or partner: Highest substitution risk. If OEMs provide the criteria, a third-party scan has less role. Dealer decisions are likely binding for warranty and liability.
- **Copart UK and salvage handlers; LiBaService24; German hazard-management firms**: Copart UK runs EV storage zones, temperature sensing and trained staff (nearly 150 staff trained to IMI Levels 2-4, sponsored piece 8 Jun 2026, vendor marketing). LiBaService24 (Swiss, co-author of the SVV annex) analyses and transports damaged packs. Copart has no Baltic yards; the nearest is Finland. Price/model: No prices found for any. Threat or partner: Partners or benchmarks for the storage and routing part of our chain. A direct competitor only if they enter the Baltics.

## Buyer map
- Insurer CASCO claims head or head of motor claims (BTA, Balta, If, ERGO, Compensa, Gjensidige, Seesam): the economic buyer and the person who bears the loss. Pays for towing, storage, expert and repair or write-off under CASCO. Needs a verdict they can defend to the claimant and the reinsurer. They hold the claim files we need for the retrospective review.
- Insurer technical or fraud/claims quality team and reinsurer: gatekeepers on liability. They will ask who pays if a cleared pack later burns. The TWAICE/Great Lakes accuracy guarantee is the precedent for insurer-backed assurance.
- LTAB and LAA: the association and the OCTA bureau. LTAB certifies experts and holds the loss-assessment methodology. Useful as a credibility route and to understand whether our output can enter an expert report. Not a payer.
- Independent loss adjusters and certified experts: they write the report that insurers rely on. Either a competitor for the assessment role or a distribution channel if we supply the battery data. Name of any EV-capable firm in Latvia: not found.
- Tow and recovery firms: first on scene and decide pack removal, container use and storage. They invoice the insurer (Allianz EV tow cost about double; Koblenz and Harz cases). A natural distribution partner and also a cost the insurer wants cut. Latvian firms with EV procedures: not found.
- Storage yards and body shops: hold the car during quarantine and repair. Need HV-qualified staff. Workshops in Latvia lack hybrid and EV mechanics (LSM, Feb 2025).
- OEM importers and dealers: hold the diagnostic tools and the repair-instruction access. The dealer diagnostic is likely binding for warranty and liability. A partner if cooperative, a blocker if not.
- Salvage dealers, importers and auction platforms: buy written-off cars and packs. No Baltic salvage data found. Buyers who benefit from a condition certificate; also potential suppliers of packs to our chain.
- Leasing and fleet operators: own many young EVs, care about residual value, and are an adjacent buyer for SoH and post-incident certificates. (Not researched in detail.)
- VUGD (fire and rescue): not a payer. Sets safety practice at the scene; no EV post-crash procedure found. A stakeholder to involve for credibility.

## Legal, safety and data issues
- Liability is the central issue. A 'safe to store or repair' verdict that is later followed by a fire exposes the assessor. A repair-shop owner told Reuters that insurers will not take the later-lawsuit risk (Reuters via devdiscourse, 20 Mar 2023; one quote). We need professional indemnity cover, a clearly limited scope ('no gross damage found at time of test', never 'safe'), and a contract that makes the insurer or expert the decision-maker. Whether PI cover exists for this risk in Latvia is unresearched.
- The SVV annex is a voluntary industry guideline (section 2 of the annex says so), not law. It says its thresholds are averages and OEM instructions prevail. Do not present the 10 mm threshold as a legal or insurer rule in the pitch.
- OEM instructions and warranty: OEM repair procedures may mandate replacement regardless of a third-party scan (AZT 14 Dec 2023). Doing HV work contrary to OEM procedure may void warranty and shift liability.
- Insurance law principle: the injured party is owed restoration of the original condition, so even cosmetic casing damage can force a large-part replacement because casing repairs are not approved (AZT PDF, Jan 2023). A scan does not change this unless the OEM approves the repair.
- Safety of the assessor: live HV work needs HV3 per the SVV annex; the minimum for scene work is HV1 plus HV2. We need qualified technicians and a safe site for a pack that could ignite later. Delayed re-ignition frequency is not known (not found).
- Transport and storage of damaged packs: damaged or defective lithium batteries fall under special carriage rules (ADR) and are likely hazardous waste when written off. This is general knowledge, not in the research; check ADR requirements and Latvian waste permits before handling any pack. The Swiss annex covers extra disposal costs for significantly damaged packs (ChemRRV), which is Swiss law and not applicable to Latvia. Latvian ELV rules (MK 135, 748, 896) as found do not address HV batteries.
- Data protection: VIN, plate, location, claim details and BMS or EDR data link to a person, so GDPR applies (my analysis, not researched). We need a data processing agreement with the insurer and a lawful basis. Who owns the BMS data (driver, OEM, insurer) is unsettled; Allianz argues battery data is the new odometer (Allianz press release 17 Oct 2023). The EU Battery Regulation passport starts Feb 2027 with SoH visible to persons with a legitimate interest, but insurer access is unconfirmed in the sources I read.
- Independence and conflict of interest: an assessor paid by the insurer, who also buys written-off packs, has a conflict. If we advise 'write off' and then buy the pack for our chain, expect challenges from claimants and insurers. Need a separation of roles and disclosure.
- Cost-of-quarantine disputes: courts scrutinise storage and quarantine fees. LG Koblenz cut a EUR 38,569.81 claim to EUR 7,596.96 total storage, and a Norwegian FinKN decision shows insurers must document causation. Our reports need to document the timeline and chain of custody to be useful as evidence.
- Unverified figures in the team brief: the German GRS tariffs of EUR 1.44/kg NMC and EUR 4.38/kg LFP could not be reproduced from any GRS price sheet I could access. Treat as unverified until someone reads the current GRS tariff. The SVV date 'Feb 2026' is wrong (signed 31 Oct 2025, posted Mar 2026).

## Fit with our battery chain
- Routing of packs the scan rejects: under the SVV criteria, packs with casing deformation of 10 mm or more, holes, water or fire involvement are excluded from further testing and disposed of. Those go to recycling or specialist handling, not second life. That is a feedstock and handling revenue line for us, but it is a cost centre for the insurer, so we must show we lower total cost. GRS tariffs for damaged packs (EUR 1.44/kg NMC, EUR 4.38/kg LFP, transport-critical packs in a safety container) are from the team brief and unverified.
- Second life and salvage: only packs that pass the electrical and structural checks can be reused. The Synetiq claim that at least 95% of cells in stored packs are undamaged is a company estimate for its yard, so do not use it to size the second-life feedstock. Salvage uplift from a certified condition report is plausible but has no supporting data.
- Take-back: insurer write-offs are a source of packs with known history, but only a small one. At the central volume of about 57 triggered assessments a year in Latvia, only a fraction of those are write-offs with a usable pack. Do not model the chain's supply on this stream.
- Testing capability reuse: our battery health testing (EUR 60-130 per car in Riga, team data) uses the same BMS/OBD read-out as the electrical step of the SVV analysis. The incremental investment is HV safety qualification, an insulation tester, a safe holding site and the insurer interface.
- Safe storage: a quarantine and holding yard for damaged packs, with fire-safe layout (UK 15 m and Swiss 2.5 m rules vary), is the scarce resource in the UK (a yard for 100 ICE cars holds about 2 quarantined EVs). A small purpose-built holding site could be the real asset. No Baltic equivalent found.
- Conflict risk (repeated): being both the assessor for the insurer and the buyer of packs needs a clear separation.
- Positioning: this is a service line that opens doors to insurers and tow firms and feeds the chain, not the main profit pool. It should be pitched as an entry channel with a pilot to prove value, not as the core revenue.

## Risks
- Demand risk: no insurer was found paying anyone for this today, and no pilot or measured saving was found. The pain is documented in the UK and Germany but not in Latvia.
- Volume risk: about 57 triggered assessments a year in Latvia (own estimate) and a few hundred in the Baltics. The AZT '2% of claims' rests on about 8 cases from 2021.
- OEM substitution: OEMs publish their own assessment criteria (the VW MEB catalogue) and hold diagnostic access. Their verdict is likely binding, which can make a third-party scan redundant.
- Detection and liability risk: no scan can prove a pack safe. A single post-clearance fire could end the business. PI cover availability and cost unknown.
- Standards risk: Thatcham/BSI, TUV-Verband, VDA and SVV are building the standards. Incumbents (DEKRA, TUV) will own the method in the large markets. We are an unproven entrant with no accreditation.
- Shrinking cost pool: OEM 24 h quarantine, VDA/GDV/ADAC guideline and court caps are already cutting excess quarantine and tow cost, the best-documented saving.
- Price pressure: Thatcham wants open, cheap diagnostics on common equipment, and insurers already pay for expert and workshop diagnostics. A premium price needs proof of avoided cost.
- Data-quality risk in our own evidence: several headline numbers are secondary, old or contradicted (AZT 2021 sample, 18% higher AZT claim cost unverified, CCC 23.1% comparison misleading, Finland total-loss figure unverified, Norway threshold wrong in detail, the team's SVV date and GRS tariffs unverified). Using them in a pitch invites challenge.
- Conflict of interest between assessing for insurers and buying packs for our chain.
- Operational risk: we would handle damaged packs (fire risk, permits, storage space, HV-qualified staff) with a small team.
- Customer concentration: Latvia has few insurers, so losing one pilot partner removes a large share of the market. Concentration also helps sales, but both effects are strong.
