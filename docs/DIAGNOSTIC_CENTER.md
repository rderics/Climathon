# Diagnostic center idea: full-body car scanner plus vehicle data (31 Oct 2026)

Multi-agent research (5 angles, a skeptic per angle, one synthesis; run wf_788e755a-9a1). Full findings and skeptic notes: `data/diagnostic_center_raw.json`. Not yet reviewed line by line by a human; marketing claims are flagged in the text.

## Verdict
Split verdict. (1) The "full body scanner that scans the battery" is NOT real as a battery test. Drive-through scanners (UVeye, Mercedes/DVS-type camera arrays) see the car's skin, tyres and underside. For an installed pack they can at most show casing damage, leaks and missing shields. I found no product, vendor document or independent data showing any drive-through scanner measures state of health (SoH), capacity, cell balance or internal faults. Real SoH numbers come from the car's data port (OBD/BMS), a short loaded drive, or a charge-load test, not from imaging. (2) The business idea underneath, "a diagnostic center that sells a decision-ready electronic report to garages", is real but crowded at the SoH layer: AVILOO, DEKRA, TUV NORD, TUV Rheinland/TWAICE, TEXA, Hella Gutmann and Mahle already sell it, and Reg. 2023/1542 Art. 14 lowers the barrier for everyone. (3) Owning a scanner lane is a bad bet for a start-up: about EUR 150k purchase per lane (trade press), no Baltic reference customer, and our volume (about 10 used-EV imports a day if the uncorroborated 3,500/yr is right) is far below what lane vendors say is needed. Recommendation: do not buy a scanner. Build a small battery-first diagnostic and triage service (OBD/BMS SoH via a licensed partner method + a human or phone underbody check + our own grade and routing), sell the report to garages, importers and leasers at a price below the retail SoH tests, and use it as the lead engine for take-back, second life and recycling. Treat the scanner as a later add-on via a partner who already owns one. Main weakness of the whole idea: the SoH method is not ours, accuracy claims by vendors are unvalidated, and we have no evidence yet that Latvian garages will pay for it.

## What a scanner can and cannot see of an installed battery
- PHYSICALLY POSSIBLE (physics, not products): cameras see only what is visible, and many EV packs sit behind underbody plates, so a camera sees the plate, not the pack. A sealed, shielded, liquid-cooled pack hides its cells from infrared and visible light. An infrared camera reads surface temperature only and cannot see through the housing; it needs an active load and a baseline, and a parked car at ambient temperature is thermally quiet (my inference; FLIR EV safety whitepaper, vendor, undated). X-ray/CT can see through the housing and show internal geometry (misalignment, deformation, swelling) but not capacity.
- SOLD BY VENDORS (marketing, not independently validated): UVeye CEO said in Aug 2023 the underbody scanner 'checks for broken EV battery cases' and battery-health sensors were still in development (Unite.ai interview, 2023-08-07; vendor claim). FreightWaves 2 Oct 2025 says thermal imaging for EV battery monitoring is 'being integrated' in the heavy-duty truck platform, availability 'expected in 2026'; no specs, no follow-up found as of 31 Oct 2026. UVeye's homepage (checked 31 Oct 2026) mentions no EV battery feature. Instavalo (Germany) says an underbody check could reveal battery damage or wrong re-installation (autohaus.de, 14 Jul 2025, vendor). Vumo snippets say it 'estimates EV battery condition' but its own product page does not mention EV batteries; its 'up to 95%' accuracy has no defined metric. Treat 'MRI for vehicles' (Hertz release wording) as marketing.
- PROVEN / PLAUSIBLY SUPPORTED: underbody cameras detect leaks, missing parts, corrosion, scrapes and obvious casing damage; the Swiss HV-battery guideline (SVV, Feb 2026, official) treats casing deformation of 10 mm or more, holes or torn casing as grounds to exclude the battery without further inspection, so a flagged casing is a real triage trigger. Even this has no independent accuracy study that I found (one forum anecdote alleges a false positive led to an unneeded replacement; unverified). Hertz's own figure: more than 97% of 500,000 scanned rentals showed no billable damage (Global Fleet, 2025-08-01), and a US House Oversight letter on AI damage billing is dated 2025-08-20, which shows false-positive and dispute risk for automated verdicts.
- CAN SEE (casing level): dents, punctures, scrapes, deformation of the pack housing, coolant/fluid leaks nearby, missing or loose shields and fasteners, corrosion, signs of repair or bad re-installation.
- CANNOT SEE: capacity or SoH, internal resistance, cell imbalance, chemistry or cell maker, fast-charge history, internal shorts, swelling inside the housing (only X-ray), insulation resistance or water ingress (needs electrical measurement), BMS software faults.
- WHAT DOES SEE INSIDE: Fraunhofer AIR full-vehicle X-ray (360 kV, tested on more than 100 private EVs, about 10 min scan and about 5 min automated evaluation) shows layer misalignment, deformation and swelling, but not SoH, has no price, no commercial availability and no announced roadworthiness use (ASNT Materials Evaluation 2026-05-01; electrive 2026-09-22). Fraunhofer XXL-CT resolves installed modules at 0.5-1 mm but is a lab facility in Furth, 'too expensive for private individuals' (Fraunhofer IIS, 2023-01-31, official). Neither fits a service-centre lane.
- WHAT MEASURES SoH: OBD/BMS read plus cloud model (AVILOO Flash about 3 min; TUV NORD BatterieCheck minutes), a short loaded drive (DEKRA about 15 min: static data plus about 100 m acceleration, model-specific parameters, claimed +/-2.5% error only at 10-30 C, mean error under 9% from -10 to 40 C, vendor slide deck), or a load during AC charging (TUV Rheinland/TWAICE via Hella Gutmann, about 60-90 min). All accuracy figures are vendor claims; I found no independent validation of any of them.
- WARNING ABOUT THE BMS NUMBER ITSELF: an unreviewed preprint (arXiv 2603.21592, 23 Mar 2026; 1,114 EVs, Hyundai/Kia/Genesis/Audi/VW only) found BMS-reported SoH correlates poorly with measured capacity (rho 0.10-0.62), and 384 vehicles expose no SoH at all. So even the OBD route has a quality problem; it is a preprint on five makers, not proof for all cars.
- OBD for 'other data': standard OBD2 readers show few generic codes on EVs; cell voltages and insulation values need OEM or advanced tools (XENTRY, ODIS, ISTA; low-authority German workshop source). Swiss guideline lists the useful BMS reads: DTCs, SoC, cell and module voltages, module temperatures, cell-voltage difference, insulation values, SoH, pyrofuse state.

## Business model options
- **A. Battery SoH + triage report sold per test to garages, used-EV importers and dealers (recommended start)** — who pays: Importers and dealers (to sell with a certificate), independent garages without EV tools, buyers of used EVs.; price points: Anchors: AVILOO partner cost GBP 35 vs GBP 95 retail (UK); TUV NORD EUR 89.90; TUV Rheinland EUR 149; ADAC EUR 79; Lithuanian blog EUR 50-120 and a Latvian-language broker guide EUR 30-80 for an OEM-tool read (commercial, undated). Our EUR 60-130 range is at the top of the OBD-based range. Hypothesis to test: EUR 50-90 retail, EUR 35-60 net to a garage that resells.; capex: Low: OBD readers or licensed boxes (about USD 1,095 per Autel EV kit; AVILOO unit rental GBP 35/month UK terms), laptop, training. No lane.; main risk: We do not own the measurement method; accuracy is vendor-claimed only; garages can buy their own tool; AVILOO or DEKRA may enter the Baltics directly; licence fees for the EU/Latvia are unknown.
- **B. Intake lane: partner or rent a scanner (UVeye-type) for importers, fleets and auctions, battery casing flag only** — who pays: Large importers, leasers, rental fleets, auction houses; OEMs through dealer programmes.; price points: Not found for Baltics. Reported per-lane USD 3,000-7,000/month (unverified); my arithmetic: a EUR 4-6k monthly lease needs roughly 30-100 paid tests at EUR 60-130 just to cover the lease, before staff, site and power.; capex: EUR 150-250k to buy (Mercedes 2026; DVS older) or EUR 4-6k+/month lease (stale); site works extra.; main risk: Volume. At the uncorroborated 3,500 imports/yr we would have about 10 cars a day, below the 40 vehicles per day a Volvo dealer case study suggests (UVeye-linked source, via search). Plus false-positive disputes, vendor failure (Proov), and the scanner adds nothing for SoH.
- **C. Remote report platform for service centres: one electronic file per car (SoH, DTCs, casing photos, tyres, our grade, recommended action) via portal/API** — who pays: Garages and service chains, possibly leasers and insurers.; price points: Subscription plus per report, like DEKRA's SaaS terms (prices not published). Garages pay small sums for expert help today (EUR 385/year hotline, 2019). Likely EUR 10-30 per report on top of the test if bundled (my estimate, untested).; capex: Low to medium: software development, API/PDF, hosting.; main risk: Data aggregation alone fails commercially (Otonomo fell from about USD 1.4B at SPAC to about USD 70M market cap at the Urgently merger, TechCrunch 2023-02-10). The report must be decision-ready and attached to a test someone pays for.
- **D. Triage-as-lead-generation: cheap or free grade for owners and garages, we earn from take-back, second-life and recycling** — who pays: We earn on packs: purchase or take-back margin, second-life resale, recycling fee. Test is subsidised.; price points: Test EUR 0-60 (to be tested). Pack values not covered by this research.; capex: Low, plus working capital for pack purchase and storage (outside this research).; main risk: Conflict of interest: a test paid for by the party who then buys the pack cheaply. Owners and garages may distrust it; needs independent-looking certification and clear separation.
- **E. Own measurement method (load test or charge-window) as a certified product** — who pays: Same as A, plus warranty and leasing partners.; price points: EUR 79-149 retail benchmarks.; capex: High: R&D, validation against lab capacity, certification (CARA/TUV-style). Not for this year.; main risk: No independent validation exists even for the big players; costly and slow; incumbents have large data sets (AVILOO 550,000+ tests).

## Legal and data issues
- Reg. (EU) 2023/1542 Art. 14: per the consolidated text on gesetze.legal (a legal-text mirror, NOT EUR-Lex; confirm on EUR-Lex before the pitch), the BMS must hold SoH and expected-lifetime data from 18 Aug 2024; any person who lawfully acquired the battery, expressly including independent operators, waste managers and third parties acting for them, gets non-discriminatory read access to those data via the BMS for residual-value assessment and reuse/repurposing/recycling; Art. 14(3) requires a BMS software reset function. Caveats: the right belongs to the battery owner or whoever acts for them, so a diagnostic centre needs an owner mandate; manufacturer IP is protected; no standard technical access method is defined. One earlier research pass wrongly described access as 'legitimate interest' under implementing acts; that wording belongs to the Annex XIII battery passport, not Art. 14. Battery passport for EV batteries reportedly from Feb 2027 (Codibly, vendor).
- Vehicle OBD/RMI access: Reg. (EU) 2018/858 Art. 61 gives independent operators OBD and repair information. Delegated Reg. (EU) 2026/699 (adopted 23 Mar 2026, in force 23 Jun 2026; Noerr summary) lets OEMs use certificates and tokens at secure gateways, with API duties phased 23 Sep 2026, 23 Dec 2026, 23 Jun 2027 and 23 Jun 2028, and adds battery diagnostics and repair info (Annex X 2.5.12/2.5.13). Security-relevant access needs SERMI certification (valid 5 years). I read this via a law-firm summary, not the primary text. Practical battery-data access is not settled.
- EU Data Act: applicable since 12 Sep 2025; connected-vehicle design duty from 12 Sep 2026. Users can authorise third parties, but most OEMs use 'extended vehicle' servers, so it does not give a garage a live OBD read.
- Personal data (my assessment, not from the research): VIN plus owner plus location of repair is personal data under GDPR; reports sent to garages need owner consent, a data processing agreement and retention rules. Needs a lawyer.
- Liability and trust: a wrong SoH or a false 'damaged casing' flag can change a car's price or trigger an unneeded pack replacement. The Hertz/UVeye billing dispute and US House Oversight letter (2025-08-20) show the reputational risk. Reports need disclaimers, a human review step for adverse findings, and insurance.
- Certification: buyers trust TUV, DEKRA and AVILOO labels. DEKRA SaaS requires trained authorised personnel with compliance checks; AVILOO reports are TUV-certified; TUV NORD and TEXA reference CARA certification. Using their method means accepting their licence terms; our own method would have no accepted standing.
- Safety: working on high-voltage vehicles requires HV training (Swiss guideline: HV1/HV2 for non-live work). X-ray (if ever used) brings radiation licensing; not relevant to the minimum version.
- Roadworthiness: the EU Roadworthiness Package (2025/0097(COD)) is in trilogue (Council approach 5 Dec 2025; EP mandate 21 May 2026, 369-126-84 per search summary; 2nd trilogue debrief 20 Oct 2026; no deal found). Parliament adds test items for battery-electric/hybrid vehicles, but no confirmed SoH capacity test and no uniform EU method. Latvia/CSDD has no battery SoH rule. This is a possible tailwind or a threat (inspection bodies could add SoH), not yet a requirement.
- UNECE GTR 22 (adopted 2022-03-09) will require on-board state-of-certified-energy monitors on new BEVs; it helps future comparability but not used imports on the road today.

## Fit with our battery business
- Lead generation: every diagnostic is a contact with a car owner, importer or garage at the moment the battery state is known. Cars with low SoH or damaged casings are the earliest signal of future pack take-back. This is the strongest fit, and it needs no scanner.
- Pack triage: our existing health test (EUR 60-130, Riga) is the same product as the OBD SoH report. The new angle is packaging it as a standard electronic file for garages (grade, damage flags, DTCs, recommended action) and routing outcomes: keep driving, repair module, replace pack, second life, recycle. Swiss guideline thresholds (deformation 10 mm or more, holes, heat above 60 C) can seed our own exclusion rules.
- Second-life sourcing: the diagnostic gives early access to packs and to history (SoH, cell spread, insulation, DTCs) that raise the value of what we later buy. Art. 14(2) explicitly covers reuse and repurposing assessment, which supports the use case, with owner mandate.
- Used-EV import channel: importers are the one concentrated buyer group (subsidy rule favours recently registered used imports). Selling them a certificate per car plus a take-back option for packs that fail is a natural bundle.
- Recycling: damaged-casing flags identify cars where a pack may be unsafe or uneconomic to repair, relevant to take-back logistics and safety.
- Conflict-of-interest warning: if we both test and buy the pack, we need transparent rules and probably an independent-looking certificate (partner method) to be trusted.
- Distraction cost: a scanner lane would tie up capital and attention away from pack take-back and second life, where our edge is.

## Minimum version we could start with
"A paid pilot of about 30-50 cars in 6-8 weeks, no scanner. (1) Method: get a licensed OBD SoH method from a partner (ask AVILOO, DEKRA, TUV NORD and TEXA for Baltic partner terms) or use our current test, and be explicit about which it is. (2) Visual check: a human underbody and tyre checklist with 6-10 phone photos taken on a ramp or lift at a partner garage, covering pack casing, shields, leaks, tyres and any warning lights. (3) Report: one PDF plus a simple link or JSON file per car with SoH, BMS/DTC findings, photos, our triage grade (green/yellow/red), and 'what to fix or do next' for the garage, signed with owner consent. (4) Customers: 2-3 used-EV importers and 3-5 independent garages in Riga; offer the first reports at a discount in exchange for feedback and a price test (EUR 50, 70, 90). (5) Track: reports sold, price accepted, how often the report changed a decision, and how many packs became take-back leads. Add a scanner only if an operator offers pay-per-scan access with a battery casing flag and enough volume."

## Risks
- Product risk: the headline idea ('scanner sees the battery') fails in front of any technical judge; pitching it as stated invites a fast rejection. Reframe around the report and triage.
- Competition and substitution: AVILOO (EUR 30M raised Feb 2026, 24-country warranty), DEKRA, TUV and the tool makers already sell SoH; garages can buy a Hella or Autel tool for roughly EUR 2.5-5k; Art. 14 gives competitors the same access rights.
- Accuracy and trust: no independent validation of any commercial SoH method; BMS-reported SoH is unreliable (preprint); our report could be wrong and damage reputation.
- Demand is unproven: no Latvian price list, no count of EV-capable garages, no evidence of what importers or leasers pay; the 3,500 import figure is unverified.
- Small market: Latvia about 13k EVs; EV-capable independents are few; Baltic total only interesting as a regional hub; subsidy scheme can change.
- Capex trap: scanner lanes need volume, OEM or large-dealer channels and long contracts; vendor risk (ProovStation insolvency, UVeye debt-funded growth); false-positive disputes.
- Data access: OEM gateways and tokens (Delegated Reg. 2026/699 phase-in through 2028), no standard technical interface for Art. 14, owner mandate required; some makes will not expose SoH.
