# Skeptic case: second-life EV batteries for grid balancing in Latvia and the Baltics, 2026–2028

*Notes current as of 30 Oct 2026. Every figure carries its date. "Cited Findings" are sourced facts or clearly labelled opinions. "Inferences" are my own reasoning or arithmetic from the cited figures. Unit note: the Baltic TSO report gives capacity prices as "€/MWh". For a capacity product this means € per MW of reserve per hour, written here as €/MW/h. BNEF, Ember and Benchmark figures are in US$ and are not converted.*

## Q1. How has the fall in new LFP cell and system prices (2023–2026) changed the economics of second-life storage?

### Takeaway
New-battery prices fell so far in 2023–2025 that second-life storage lost its generic cost advantage. In Europe (2025–26), cells and packs are only about 35–40% of the cost of a turnkey system, so even free used batteries can cut only that share, and second life adds testing, integration and lifetime penalties of its own. Published break-even studies say second-life batteries must cost no more than about 40–60% of a new battery. At the 2025 stationary-pack price of $70/kWh, that means roughly $28–42/kWh, which is close to what second-life firms were already paying for used packs in 2024. Second life is still competitive only in niches: packs used as-is, captive supply, behind-the-meter sites, and off-grid or developing markets.

### Cited Findings
**New-battery price collapse**
- The average Li-ion pack price fell to **$108/kWh in 2025** (−8% y/y). **Stationary-storage packs fell to $70/kWh** (−45% y/y) and were the cheapest segment for the first time. LFP packs averaged $81/kWh. European prices were 56% above China's. BNEF expects a further decline in 2026, though rising raw-material prices push the other way. (BNEF, 9 Dec 2025) — [BNEF](https://about.bnef.com/insights/clean-energy/lithium-ion-battery-pack-prices-fall-to-108-per-kilowatt-hour)
- **Turnkey BESS** averaged **$117/kWh globally in 2025**, down from $169/kWh in 2024 (−31%), after a −40% drop from 2023 to 2024. 2-hour systems averaged $124/kWh and 4-hour systems $110/kWh. Regional prices: **Europe $177/kWh (−37% y/y)**, China $73/kWh, US $219/kWh. (BNEF Energy Storage Systems Cost Survey, 10 Dec 2025) — [Energy-Storage.news, 16 Dec 2025](https://www.energy-storage.news/?p=92133)
- Ember put average **all-in project capex at $125/kWh** (4h+ systems, outside China and the US, Oct 2025): about $75/kWh for core equipment and about $50/kWh for installation and connection, plus grid connection fees of $30–100/kWh. Shorter-duration systems cost about 10–15% more. Markets with tariffs or local-content rules pay a core-equipment premium of $100/kWh or more. — [Energy-Storage.news, 16 Dec 2025](https://www.energy-storage.news/?p=92133)
- **2026 rebound in cell prices.** China 314Ah LFP storage cells rose from CNY 0.300/Wh (~$42/kWh, late Oct 2025) to CNY 0.365/Wh (20 Apr 2026), about +22%. Lithium carbonate went from CNY 73,550/t (Oct 2025) to CNY 181,500/t (26 Jan 2026). 2h DC-side system prices rose from CNY 0.41 to 0.49/Wh. — [ESS News, 22 Apr 2026](https://www.ess-news.com/2026/04/22/chinas-314-ah-storage-cell-prices-climb-more-than-20-in-six-months/)
- In July 2026, 314Ah cells were CNY 0.360/Wh (−4% m/m), against an estimated production cost of about CNY 0.312/Wh. InfoLink expects lithium carbonate to ease to CNY 110–130k/t in H1 2027 on a possible surplus. — [SolarQuarter, 26 Aug 2026](https://solarquarter.com/2026/08/26/lithium-carbonate-prices-drop-10-as-energy-storage-cell-costs-decline/)
- In September 2026, 314Ah LFP cells were **$54.21/kWh EXW China and $62.97/kWh CIF Europe**. From 1 Sep 2026 China applies a 2% consumption tax on Li-ion batteries, and it has removed export tax rebates. (Benchmark, 9 Oct 2026) — [Benchmark Cell Price Review Q3 2026](https://source.benchmarkminerals.com/article/lithium-cost-pass-through-drives-volatile-quarter-for-energy-storage-cells-cell-price-review-q3-2026)
- CATL sells 587Ah LFP storage cells online at **CNY 435/kWh (~$65/kWh)**, minimum order 608 kWh, with a 5-year warranty (15 Sep 2026). — [CarNewsChina](https://carnewschina.com/?p=266035)

**What it did to second life**
- **Cactos** (Finland) mainly repurposed EV batteries in late 2022. In **January 2024 it said new batteries were now more economical** than repurposed ones. Second-life firm **Stabl** put its own system cost at **€400–600/kWh**, which the article calls several times first-life BESS prices (the article itself notes the comparison is loose). A North American source said **recyclers pay ~$8/kWh** for used batteries and **second-life firms ~$30/kWh**, and that used-battery prices must fall to compete. (Energy-Storage.news, ~Apr 2024) — [Energy-Storage.news](https://www.energy-storage.news/battery-price-falls-threaten-second-life-model-as-fenecon-opens-assembly-plant-in-germany/)
- Circular Energy Storage reported that **used-battery prices reached $235/kWh in late 2022**, about double what major carmakers paid for new batteries. (Reuters, 26 Jun 2023) — [EnergyNow/Reuters](https://energynow.ca/2023/06/a-second-life-for-ev-batteries-depends-how-long-the-first-is/)
- **China:** in 2020–2022, second-life (echelon) cells cost at least 30% less than new cells. After lithium fell, **second-life cells, once processing is paid for, are priced "very close" to new storage cells**, while their **remaining usable cycles are about one-third to one-half** of a new storage battery's. (36Kr, 6 Aug 2026) — [36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/3927419074745985)
- Some industrial firms are dropping second-life plans in favour of new storage. Volytica's MD says second-life prices are low because buyers price in the risk of short life and weak performance. (energate, 6 Aug 2024) — [energate](https://www.energate-messenger.com/news/246052/price-pressure-slows-down-second-life-batteries)
- Market share: second life accounts for **<0.1% of grid-scale BESS projects in North America and 0.2% in Europe** (Rho Motion database, cited 5 Sep 2025). — [Benchmark](https://source.benchmarkminerals.com/article/how-is-the-second-life-battery-market-playing-out)
- Opinion: falling LFP prices have pushed some second-life packs close to cost parity with new cells in certain markets. No figures given. (Battery Burn Book newsletter, 27 May 2026) — [Battery Burn Book](https://batteryburnbook.beehiiv.com/p/second-life-batteries)

**Break-even and cost-comparison studies**
- **Mathews et al. (Applied Energy 269, 2020).** Utility PV plus 4h storage. Second-life batteries **must cost less than ~60% of the new price to break even** (~$125/kWh against a $209/kWh new module). To beat a new system under an 85–20% state-of-charge (SOC) window they must cost less than 80%. At 50% of the new price, the benefit-cost ratio (BCR) is 0.78 (60% end-of-life threshold) and 0.47 (70% threshold), against 0.93 and 0.76 for new batteries. — [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03216); [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/appene/v269y2020ics0306261920306395.html)
- **Steckel, Kendall & Ambrose (Applied Energy 300, 2021).** Second-life utility BESS has a levelized cost of storage (**LCOS) of $234–278/MWh against $211/MWh for new**, even though it costs **64.3–78.9% of a new system upfront**. Results are highly sensitive to the discount rate, depth of discharge and repurposing costs. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/appene/v300y2021ics0306261921007212.html)
- **PNNL (Gautam et al., 13 Aug 2025).** For EV charging stations, second-life batteries "become financially attractive when their cost is **40% or lower**" than new. — [PNNL](https://www.pnnl.gov/publications/techno-economic-assessments-second-life-batteries-electric-vehicle-charging-stations)
- **Bach, Onori, Reichelstein & Zhuang (Stanford GSB WP 4203, 2024).** Used **LFP packs keep more than 40% of a new battery's market value**, which makes a strong case for repurposing LFP. For nickel-cobalt (NCX) chemistries the case is only marginal in the US, and in China owners are better off recycling immediately. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ecl/stabus/4203.html)
- **Moser et al. (World Electric Vehicle Journal 17:66, 30 Jan 2026).** Second-life packs can cost **as little as $23/kWh**. Buying the end-of-first-life battery is about **56% of repurposing cost**. A Phoenix pilot of about 80 kWh reported **20–30% lower upfront cost** than comparable new systems. — [MDPI PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/wevj/wevj-17-00066/article_deploy/wevj-17-00066.pdf)
- China's second-life market (qualitative): Grade A LFP cells see firm demand as raw material for second-life storage, and Grade B cells win share in low-end applications on price. No price levels given. (SMM, 4 Jun 2026) — [SMM](https://news.metal.com/newscontent/103938861-lithium-carbonate-pullback-dragged-down-cost-side-second-life-application-market-saw-slight-price-increases-with-categor)

### Inferences
- **The cost second life can displace has shrunk.** European turnkey BESS was $177/kWh in 2025, while storage cells cost about $55–65/kWh (Sept 2026) and stationary packs $70/kWh (2025). Cells or packs are therefore only about **35–40% of a European turnkey system**. Free used batteries could save at most that share, and second life adds costs elsewhere: testing, extra power electronics, larger footprint, more O&M and replacements (see Q3).
- **Applying the published thresholds to 2025–26 prices.** A 40–60% threshold (PNNL 2025; Mathews 2020) on a $70/kWh stationary pack gives a **maximum used-pack price of about $28–42/kWh**. That brackets the ~$30/kWh second-life firms were paying in 2024 and sits above Moser's "as low as $23/kWh". Little or nothing is left for testing and repurposing. Caveat: the thresholds come from US cases with different balance-of-system costs and duty cycles, so this is an order-of-magnitude check, not a model result.
- **LFP is squeezed from both sides.** Bach et al. imply sellers of used LFP packs can ask more than 40% of new value, while PNNL says buyers need 40% or less. The seller's reservation price and the buyer's break-even meet at about the same point. For NMC packs, the recycling value competes with reuse instead.
- **Steckel's result is now worse.** In 2021, a 21–36% upfront saving still gave an 11–32% *higher* LCOS for second life. With battery hardware now a smaller share of system cost, the achievable upfront saving shrinks further.
- **The 2026 rebound is a modest tailwind, not a reversal.** The +22% cell-price rebound and China's tax changes help second life a little, but InfoLink expects lithium to ease in 2027. The 2022 episode also shows that when new prices spike, used-pack prices spike too ($235/kWh in late 2022). A commodity spike therefore does not automatically favour second life.

### Gaps
- I found no 2025–2026 European price quotes for used EV packs by chemistry and state of health (SoH). SMM's Chinese second-life weekly reviews give direction only, not levels.
- The conclusions of Martinez-Laserna et al., "Battery second life: Hype, hope or reality?" (Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews 93:701–718, 2018), were not retrievable beyond metadata — [AAU record](https://vbn.aau.dk/en/publications/battery-second-life-hype-hope-or-reality-a-critical-review-of-the). No full-text critical review from 2023–2026 was accessed.
- I found no 2025–2026 LCOS comparison of second-life against new batteries specifically for frequency-balancing duty. The IDTechEx analysis is paywalled.

## Q2. Which second-life storage companies or programmes failed, scaled back or pivoted (2023–2026), and why?

### Takeaway
In the trade press I found few headline bankruptcies of pure second-life firms. The dominant pattern is a quiet retreat: switching to new or never-used automotive batteries, sales of second-life units, mergers into niches such as EV charging, and, in China, a state policy reversal that has effectively ended large-scale second-life storage. The reasons given are consistent: the collapse in new-battery prices, integration and BMS complexity, safety and consistency concerns, and scarce, heterogeneous supply.

### Cited Findings
- **Cactos (Finland)** moved from mainly repurposed EV batteries (late 2022) to saying new batteries were more economical (January 2024). — [Energy-Storage.news, ~Apr 2024](https://www.energy-storage.news/battery-price-falls-threaten-second-life-model-as-fenecon-opens-assembly-plant-in-germany/)
- **Fenecon** opened a 24,000 m², ~$30m second-life assembly plant in Bavaria (Apr 2024). Its C&I units (82–1,288 kWh) **use only new EV batteries from German premium carmakers** that never reached the road for manufacturing or warranty reasons. — [Energy-Storage.news](https://www.energy-storage.news/battery-price-falls-threaten-second-life-model-as-fenecon-opens-assembly-plant-in-germany/). In June 2023 Fenecon's Zimmerling said there was "as good as no market for second-life batteries" but predicted a "tsunami" within five years. — [EnergyNow/Reuters](https://energynow.ca/2023/06/a-second-life-for-ev-batteries-depends-how-long-the-first-is/)
- **Stena sold its second-life arm BatteryLoop** to Repono; it was unclear whether Repono would keep the approach. A senior director at a second-life company said: "A lot of people are dropping out of the second life energy storage space." ### Inferences
- The firms that survive do so by (a) quietly switching to never-used or new automotive batteries (Fenecon, partly Voltfang), (b) retreating to small C&I or EV-charging niches (Evyon/Elywhere, Connected Energy's historic focus), or (c) vertical integration with a recycler or OEM that controls supply (Redwood–GM, Toyota). **Merchant grid-scale balancing is the segment most clearly abandoned.**
- China is the largest real-world test of grid-scale second life (e.g., a 208 MWh station at Kunshan, 2020, per [36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/3927419074745985)). Its retreat was driven at least as much by safety and consistency as by cost, which is directly relevant to a Baltic investor's insurer and TSO.
- "Failure" in this sector seldom shows up as bankruptcy. It shows up as a product line that quietly stops using used batteries. An investor should ask any second-life vendor what share of its shipped MWh in 2025–26 was genuinely used EV batteries.

- I could not confirm whether ECO STOR's utility-scale projects use new batteries. The fetched articles are silent.

## Q3. Which technical risks dominate?

### Takeaway
The dominant technical risks are heterogeneity (cell-to-cell, pack-to-pack and design-to-design), unknown usage history that leads to unpredictable "knee" ageing, BMS and integration complexity (including standards that need OEM cooperation), and the time and cost of testing. Degradation under balancing duty is less settled than either side claims. Frequency-regulation duty can be gentle on *new* cells, but second-life modules need conservative SOC windows and still hit knees unpredictably, which raises the cost per usable, prequalifiable MW.

### Cited Findings
- **Heterogeneity and safety.** Retired cells vary widely in ageing, internal resistance and self-discharge, even within one batch. Aged cells can cause voltage imbalance, overheating, rapid capacity loss and, in severe cases, thermal runaway. (36Kr, 6 Aug 2026) — [36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/3927419074745985)
- **BMS integration.** Battery management systems struggle to combine batteries of different models, chemistries and SoH. Stabl's CEO said in August 2023 the sector would "struggle" with performance for this reason. — [Energy-Storage.news, ~Apr 2024](https://www.energy-storage.news/battery-price-falls-threaten-second-life-model-as-fenecon-opens-assembly-plant-in-germany/)
- **Standards** (Dalhousie University report for Canada's NRC, 23 Apr 2025):
  - UL 1974 requires grading and assembling systems from same-grade batteries, and **does not allow different EV battery designs in one system**.
  - Units with different voltage, capacity or SoH need per-unit power converters, and DC coupling adds cost and space.
  - **IEC 63330/63338 require repurposers to work directly with the original manufacturers**, which the report flags as a significant hurdle and potential conflict of interest.
  - [Dalhousie report](https://resl.me.dal.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/nrc-dal-2nd-life-ancillary-svc-year-1-report-final-3.pdf)
- **Testing time and cost.** A proposed qualification protocol takes **about 6–9 hours per cell**. Test methods were designed for first-life batteries, and historical data is often missing. Full automation could cut repurposing cost by up to 97% and time by 85% (a modelled estimate). EV usage data may be personal data under GDPR. (Moser et al., 30 Jan 2026) — [MDPI PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/wevj/wevj-17-00066/article_deploy/wevj-17-00066.pdf)
- **Unpredictable ageing.** Second-life Nissan Leaf modules ran **at least 2,033 equivalent full cycles before their ageing knee**. The authors conclude that **capacity and internal-resistance tests alone are not enough to predict second-life durability**. (Braco et al., Journal of Energy Storage 32:101695, 2020) — [UPNA repository](https://academica-e.unavarra.es/handle/2454/39372). An earlier paper found Leaf modules "highly heterogeneous" in capacity. (Braco et al., IEEE EEEIC 2019) — [UPNA](https://academica-e.unavarra.es/handle/2454/36738)
- **Remaining life.** Remaining usable cycles are about **one-third to one-half** of a new storage battery's, and calendar life is short (China industry assessment, 6 Aug 2026). — [36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/3927419074745985)
- **Duty cycle matters** (Mathews et al., 2020):
  - An aggressive 95–15% SOC window reached 70% end-of-life in **3.9 years**; a gentle 65–15% window in 7.5 years; a new battery in 11.4 years.
  - After 10 years, new batteries keep about 75% of capacity and second-life batteries about 63%.
  - Extending battery life was the strongest financial lever.
  - [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03216)
- **Evidence specific to balancing duty is thin.** The only dedicated studies of second-life degradation in ancillary services cover frequency regulation, and the standard Sandia (SNL) test cycle "underrepresents" today's more aggressive regulation signals. (Dalhousie, 23 Apr 2025) — [Dalhousie report](https://resl.me.dal.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/nrc-dal-2nd-life-ancillary-svc-year-1-report-final-3.pdf)
- **New cells under frequency regulation** (Ohrelius et al., Energies 16:3003, 2023):
  - 920 equivalent full cycles in 28 weeks cost **4% of capacity**, a projected ~12-year life in this duty.
  - Combining frequency regulation with peak shaving cut life to 92% capacity by **36%**.
  - The authors only *suggest*, without testing, that partly aged cells could serve frequency regulation.
  - [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/16/7/3003/xml)
- **Operational burden.** Redwood says its 63 MWh second-life system needs regular battery replacements and a larger footprint (5 Sep 2025). — [Benchmark](https://source.benchmarkminerals.com/article/how-is-the-second-life-battery-market-playing-out)
- **Supply quality and quantity**
  - Melin (Circular Energy Storage) calls the 80% retirement threshold "arbitrary". Batteries outlast the assumed 8–10 years, the lack of old EV batteries "shows no sign of easing", and about 20% of UK vehicles disappear or are exported (26 Jun 2023). — [EnergyNow/Reuters](https://energynow.ca/2023/06/a-second-life-for-ev-batteries-depends-how-long-the-first-is/)
  - Rebattex estimates "up to 180 GWh could become accessible" in Europe by 2035 (15 Sep 2026). — [EU Circular Economy Platform](https://circulareconomy.europa.eu/platform/fr/node/10312)
- **Provenance risk.** Element Energy's stock is reportedly fire-recall LG modules. — [Energy-Storage.news](https://energy-storage.news/second-life-firm-element-energy-using-recalled-lg-batteries-says-source)
- **Counterpoint, using the OEM BMS as-is.** B2U installs packs unmodified on their original BMSs and uses cabinet controllers to switch strings so weak packs do not limit strong ones. The CEO claims this is "virtually eliminating repurposing costs" (company claim). — [DCD](https://datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/batteries-from-electric-vehicles-provide-25mwh-of-storage-to-california-grid)

### Inferences
- For FCR and aFRR (fast, shallow, frequent cycling), the main threat to second life is probably **not average capacity fade**. It is (a) resistance growth that limits deliverable power, (b) unpredictable knees, and (c) the derating needed to cope with heterogeneity. Each of these **reduces the MW that can be prequalified per installed kWh**, and balancing revenue is paid per MW.
- Per-cell testing at 6–9 hours scales badly to MWh-scale projects unless testing is done at pack level or the B2U as-is architecture is used. But the as-is approach locks in heterogeneity and OEM BMS dependence, and the UL 1974 / IEC 63330 routes need OEM cooperation that a Latvian repurposer is unlikely to get.
- Mathews' gentle-SOC result means a second-life asset has to be **oversized and run conservatively** to last. That raises the effective cost per usable kWh and per MW and eats into any purchase-price discount.

### Gaps
- No Baltic-specific data on energy throughput (equivalent full cycles per year) for FCR and aFRR provision, which is needed to translate duty into degradation.
- I found no documented European second-life BESS fire. My sources do not establish that the 16 Apr 2021 Beijing explosion involved second-life batteries; Benchmark asserts a link only to "a spate" of 2021 Chinese fires.
- No sourced round-trip-efficiency comparison between second-life and new systems.

## Q4. Which market risks are specific to the Baltic balancing market?

### Takeaway
The high-price window is closing fast. In Latvia, Jan–Jun 2026 average capacity prices were **22–87% below 2025 averages**, depending on product. Lithuania's battery fleet grew from about 0.7 GW (June 2026) to about 1.2 GW (Oct 2026) and could pass 2 GW by end-2026. That compares with a total Baltic reserve requirement of about 1.6 GW (both directions, mostly mFRR), including only **28 MW of FCR and about 100–120 MW of aFRR**. A second-life project that needs 12–24 months for sourcing, testing, certification and prequalification would probably come online in 2027–28, into a saturated market where revenue increasingly comes from arbitrage, which is the duty second life handles worst.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
**Revenue collapse (my arithmetic from the report's averages).** These are upper bounds that assume the asset wins every hour at the average clearing price, ignoring energy-reservoir limits, availability and fees. The 2025 average covers only Feb–Dec 2025 and includes the extreme early months, and the Jan–Jun 2026 average is affected by seasonality.

| Product (1 MW, Latvia) | 2025 average | Jan–Jun 2026 average | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symmetric aFRR (up + down) | 167.67 €/MW/h ≈ €1.47m/yr | 40.24 €/MW/h ≈ €0.35m/yr | **−76%** |
| FCR | ≈ €0.77m/yr | ≈ €0.40m/yr | −48% |
| Symmetric mFRR | ≈ €0.56m/yr | ≈ €0.36m/yr | −36% |

At Finland's Jan–Jun 2026 aFRR prices the same calculation gives about €82k/MW/yr, a plausible end point if prices converge with the neighbours.

**Oversupply is already visible in FCR and aFRR.**
- Prequalified FCR is 405 MW against 28 MW of demand (~14×). Battery-only prequalified FCR (EE 76 + LV 40 + LT 40 = 156 MW) is ~5.6× demand.
- Prequalified aFRR up is 1,629 MW against ~120 MW (~14×). Estonian and Latvian batteries alone (477 MW) are ~4× demand.
- Lithuania's battery fleet by itself (~1.2 GW in Oct 2026) exceeds the whole upward reserve requirement (~604 mFRR + ~120 aFRR + 28 FCR ≈ 750 MW).
- The products where battery speed matters most (FCR and aFRR) add up to **only about 150 MW per direction for the whole Baltic block**.

**Free entry pushes prices down to the cost of new LFP.** A new 2h battery costs roughly $0.28–0.48m per MW, based on Ember's $125/kWh plus 10–15% for short duration plus $30–100/kWh grid fees, or BNEF's $177/kWh for Europe. At Jan–Jun 2026 Latvian aFRR prices such a battery would still pay back in a few years, which explains the rush of new projects. Entry will continue until prices fall to the level where new LFP earns only its cost of capital. **The market will not pay a premium for second life**, so second life survives only if its cost per qualified MW-year is below that of new LFP. Q1 and Q3 suggest it is not.

**Timing.** Aurora's IRR compression, combined with second-life lead times for sourcing, testing, certification, insurance and prequalification, means a second-life project reaching commercial operation in 2027–28 misses the premium window. It would then rely on arbitrage, where round-trip efficiency, cycle life and warranty matter most and second life is weakest.

**Upside risks to this bear case.**
- TSO zero-price resources leave the market by November 2028 at the latest (AST's 40 MW, Kiisa's 250/40 MW, Energy Cells).
- The report's scenario says a 25% cut in TSO resources would raise costs by 51%. That scenario uses the Oct 2025–Jun 2026 supply stack, so the GW-scale battery inflow may absorb much of the effect.
- Seasonal June scarcity and the persistent premium over neighbouring markets show prices will not fall in a straight line.

### Gaps
- BESS prequalification details for the Baltic market were not retrieved: minimum bid size, energy-reservoir and duration rules, test procedures, aggregation and re-prequalification after degradation.
- I found **no public evidence of any second-life battery prequalified** in the Baltic balancing market.
- No forward price curves or analyst forecasts for 2027–2028 Baltic capacity prices beyond Aurora's qualitative trajectory.

## Q5. Which regulatory and liability risks deter investors?

### Takeaway
Under Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, **whoever repurposes a battery and places it on the market is legally its manufacturer** (Art. 38(11)). That brings compliance, new labelling, a new battery passport linked to the original (mandatory from 18 Feb 2027), extended-producer-responsibility (EPR) duties and possibly a new battery category. Meanwhile **the original OEM is not liable for breaches caused by software uploaded later** (Art. 14(3)). Key secondary rules (passport data access, used-versus-waste criteria) were still not final in October 2026. Insurers have grown more cautious about battery fire risk and often exclude serial defects, which fits badly with heterogeneous assets that have no tier-1 warranty.

### Cited Findings
**Regulation (EU) 2023/1542** (text retrieved via FAOLEX) — [Regulation text](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/eur223915.pdf)
- **Art. 38(11):** economic operators that prepare batteries for re-use or repurposing, repurpose or remanufacture them, and place them on the market or put them into service "**shall be considered to be manufacturers**".
- **Art. 45:** such operators must ensure that examination, performance testing, packing and shipment follow "adequate quality control and safety instructions". The battery must comply with this Regulation and other Union law, "taking into account the fact that … the battery might fall under a **different battery category**."
- **Art. 44(b)–(c):** an importer or distributor that modifies a battery, or changes its purpose, is treated as a manufacturer.
- **Art. 13(9):** processed batteries must "bear new labels", with the change of status shown through the QR code.
- **Art. 14(2):** requires "read-only access" to battery-management-system data on the Annex VII parameters, including for repurposing.
- **Art. 14(3):** the BMS must include a software reset function, and the **original manufacturer is not liable for breaches attributable to software uploaded later**.
- **Arts. 7(5), 8(4) and 10(4)** exempt previously placed batteries from certain carbon-footprint, recycled-content and performance requirements, a mild advantage for second life.
- Definitions: Art. 3(1)(31) repurposing; Art. 3(1)(32) remanufacturing (restoring at least 90% of rated capacity); Art. 3(1)(16) "placing on the market" means first making available on the EU market.

**EPR, passport and due diligence**
- **EPR:** the Regulation "treats the first marketer of a repurposed battery as the producer" for EPR purposes and requires evidence, such as SoH testing, that a prepared waste battery is no longer waste. Battery passports apply from **18 Feb 2027** to EV batteries and industrial batteries above 2 kWh. **Due-diligence obligations were delayed to 18 Aug 2027.** (BSI, 2026) — [BSI](https://www.bsigroup.com/en-GB/insights-and-media/insights/blogs/the-second-life-of-ev-batteries/)
- **Passport** (Commission FAQ as summarised on 11 Oct 2026; guidance, not binding):
  - A repurposed or remanufactured battery **needs a new passport linked to the previous one**. A simple repair does not.
  - A battery placed on the market before February 2027 and repurposed afterwards also needs a passport.
  - The responsible operator must verify itself in the passport registry.
  - The act on who may read restricted passport data was due on 18 Aug 2026 and **had not been adopted**; a draft is expected in Oct/Nov 2026.
  - [Battery-Tech Network](https://battery-tech.net/the-eu-battery-passport-belongs-to-whoever-finishes-the-battery-four-months-before-it-becomes-mandatory/)

**Waste status, standards and liability**
- Art. 73(3) provides for an implementing act on telling used batteries from waste. Annex XIV sets documentation for cross-border shipments. Waste batteries may only be prepared for repurposing by specialised, waste-qualified companies. **No harmonised standards** yet exist, and second-life conformity builds on the original conformity assessment. The paper flags uncertainty over accident liability. (Moser et al., 30 Jan 2026) — [MDPI PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/wevj/wevj-17-00066/article_deploy/wevj-17-00066.pdf)
- CEPS (Moreschi & Rizos, 5 May 2026) calls for "more clarity … regarding the liability framework governing responsibilities for batteries entering repurposing pathways", and for harmonised SoH and SoC methods. — [EU Circular Economy Platform](https://circulareconomy.europa.eu/platform/fr/node/9880)
- Rebattex (15 Sep 2026): diagnostics, valuation, regulation, logistics and cross-border processes are fragmented, and "regulation alone does not create functioning markets". — [EU Circular Economy Platform](https://circulareconomy.europa.eu/platform/fr/node/10312)

**Insurance and finance**
- On BESS generally (1 Jun 2026):
  - Thermal runaway is "the most critical loss path". Recent fires have reduced some insurers' risk appetite and made premiums uneven.
  - **Serial or design defects are often excluded** from standard policies, and fire sublimits may be too low.
  - "Warranty promises are only as good as the credit standing behind them."
  - **Merchant projects get lower gearing, shorter loan tenors and higher margins.**
  - [ESS News](https://www.ess-news.com/2026/06/01/when-the-battery-becomes-a-balance-sheet-question/)
- An insurance adviser proposes insured warranties to make second life bankable. The provider has paid out on one energy-storage claim, and an approved O&M contract is required. (Promotional, 7 Aug 2025) — [EV Infrastructure News](https://www.evinfrastructurenews.com/second-life-batteries/breaking-the-second-life-battery-bankability-block)
- In China, owners and EPC contractors are reluctant to take on the safety-compliance risk of second-life batteries and exclude them from bids (6 Aug 2026). — [36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/3927419074745985)

### Inferences
- The manufacturer rule puts product compliance and liability, EPR fees, new labels and passport set-up **on each repurposed battery**. These costs scale with the number of batteries. Many small EV packs carry more of them per MWh than a few large new containers.
- Art. 45(2)'s "different battery category" probably turns an EV battery reused for stationary storage into an industrial or stationary battery subject to the stationary-storage safety requirements. **I could not verify from the fetched text** which articles (e.g., Art. 12 and its annex tests) apply. This needs legal review.
- Art. 14 read-only access helps with diagnosis but gives no control. A repurposer that reflashes or replaces the BMS takes the liability (Art. 14(3)).
- Finance: Baltic revenues are largely merchant, and second life has no OEM-backed warranty. That combination puts a second-life project in the **worst corner of the financing matrix**: low gearing, high margins and serial-defect exclusions. A higher cost of capital can wipe out the thin capex advantage.
- Latvia has few retired EV packs of its own (unverified; see Gaps), so supply would probably be imported. If the batteries count as waste, shipments carry waste-shipment documentation and require waste-qualified handlers.

### Gaps
- No quantified insurance-premium or lender-margin difference between second-life and new BESS.
- No Latvian implementation details: national EPR scheme handling of repurposed batteries, or market-surveillance practice.
- Not verified: whether Art. 12 stationary-storage safety requirements apply to repurposed batteries.
- Latvian EV fleet size and expected retirement volumes were not retrieved.

## Q6. Under what conditions does the idea still work? (Break-even analyses, kill criteria and survival conditions)

### Takeaway
The published break-evens broadly agree. Second life needs to cost **no more than about 40–60% of the new-battery price** (about $28–42/kWh at 2025 stationary-pack prices), **last at least about 7 years** in service, be **run gently**, and **avoid per-cell repurposing**. Those conditions are met mainly with captive, homogeneous supply (OEM, recycler or fleet), as-is pack designs, and behind-the-meter or off-grid niches. For a standalone, merchant Baltic balancing project in 2026–2028, most of the kill criteria below are likely to be triggered.

### Cited Findings
- **Mathews et al. (2020):** second-life batteries must cost less than ~60% of new to break even. With gentle 65–15% SOC operation, a 60% end-of-life threshold and a price of 50% of new, the second-life BCR matches the new-battery value (0.93). Extending life is the strongest lever. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03216)
- **PNNL (Aug 2025):** attractive at no more than 40% of new cost (EV charging stations). — [PNNL](https://www.pnnl.gov/publications/techno-economic-assessments-second-life-batteries-electric-vehicle-charging-stations)
- **Steckel et al. (2021):** LCOS is higher for second life, and sensitive to discount rate, depth of discharge and repurposing cost. The authors suggest incentives at least equal to those for first-life storage if society values second life. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/appene/v300y2021ics0306261921007212.html)
- **CrossBoundary (30 Jul 2025):** mini-grid pilots in South Africa, Nigeria and DR Congo showed **11–28% lower upfront cost** and up to 18% lower lifetime cost, **but only if batteries last at least 7 years**. Replacement every 6 years erases the savings over 15–18 years, and returns depend on field management (charge control, temperature, depth of discharge). — [CrossBoundary](https://crossboundary.com/second-life-batteries-cost-savings-for-mini-grids-africa-rural-electrification/)
- **Bach et al. (2024):** strong economic case for LFP repurposing; marginal for NCX in the US; recycle NCX immediately in China. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ecl/stabus/4203.html)
- **Benchmark (5 Sep 2025):** deployment has been led by players with **direct access to end-of-life cells** (recyclers, automakers). In smaller C&I applications, cost advantages "may outweigh technical drawbacks" (analyst view). — [Benchmark](https://source.benchmarkminerals.com/article/how-is-the-second-life-battery-market-playing-out)
- **Moser et al. (2026):** automation could cut repurposing cost by up to 97%. A Phoenix pilot showed 20–30% lower upfront cost. The paper's forecast that second life will make up 18% of European storage installations by 2028 (14 GWh of 78 GWh) **conflicts with the actual 0.2% share** of European grid-scale projects reported by Rho Motion via Benchmark (Sep 2025). — [MDPI PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/wevj/wevj-17-00066/article_deploy/wevj-17-00066.pdf); [Benchmark](https://source.benchmarkminerals.com/article/how-is-the-second-life-battery-market-playing-out)
- **Niche evidence:**
  - B2U: as-is packs, 25 MWh, operating since 2020. — [DCD](https://datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/batteries-from-electric-vehicles-provide-25mwh-of-storage-to-california-grid)
  - Elywhere/Evyon: storage plus EV charging at weak-grid sites (May 2025). — [ESS News](https://www.ess-news.com/?p=5024)
  - Insured warranties are promoted for SME-scale sites such as charging stations and depots, where UK fleet-depot grid connections can wait up to 15 years (Aug 2025). — [EV Infrastructure News](https://www.evinfrastructurenews.com/second-life-batteries/breaking-the-second-life-battery-bankability-block)
- **Effect of new-battery cost shocks:** markets with tariffs or local-content rules pay a core-equipment premium of $100/kWh or more (Ember, Oct 2025). — [Energy-Storage.news](https://www.energy-storage.news/?p=92133). But in 2022 used-pack prices also rose to ~2× new. — [EnergyNow/Reuters](https://energynow.ca/2023/06/a-second-life-for-ev-batteries-depends-how-long-the-first-is/)

### Inferences

#### Kill criteria (my synthesis; stop if any one is true)
1. **Cost gate.** All-in delivered second-life DC capacity (pack + logistics + testing + rework + extra power electronics and footprint), per usable kWh over its expected life, is **not at least ~40–50% below a new LFP DC block**. As of 2025–26, new cells and packs cost about $55–70/kWh, and European turnkey systems $177/kWh. In practice: if used packs cost **more than ~$30–40/kWh** before repurposing, stop. (PNNL; Mathews; Steckel; BNEF; Benchmark)
2. **Life gate.** Duty-cycle tests on representative packs show **under ~7 years to usable end-of-life** (CrossBoundary), or knees before ~2,000 equivalent full cycles (Braco), at the intended Baltic duty. Remaining-cycle evidence of one-third to one-half of new (36Kr) needs a matching price discount.
3. **Price gate (Baltic).** Analysis shows 2027–28 FCR and aFRR capacity prices converging towards Nordic levels (Jan–Jun 2026 Finland: FCR ~9 €/MW/h; aFRR up + down ~9.4 €/MW/h). At that point a balancing-only case dies, and the project depends on arbitrage, where second life is weakest. (BBCM report; Aurora)
4. **Timing gate.** The project cannot reach commercial operation *and* prequalification by about mid-2027. Aurora's 2029-entry IRR is 6–13% even for new batteries.
5. **Bankability gate.** No insurer will cover thermal runaway and serial defects on workable terms, or no lender will provide meaningful debt without an OEM-backed or insured performance warranty. (ESS News, Jun 2026)
6. **Supply gate.** The project cannot contract several MWh of **homogeneous** packs (same OEM design and chemistry, documented history and BMS data). UL 1974 does not allow mixing designs, and IEC 63330/63338 need OEM cooperation. (Dalhousie 2025)
7. **Compliance gate.** The repurposer cannot meet manufacturer duties (Arts. 38(11) and 45), EPR registration and linked passports from 18 Feb 2027 at a cost per battery that preserves the discount. (Regulation 2023/1542; BSI; Battery-Tech Network)
8. **Chemistry gate.** If the packs are NMC/NCX and recycling value is at least as high as reuse value, recycle instead of repurposing. (Bach et al.)

#### Conditions under which it can still work (most must hold at once)
1. **Captive, cheap, homogeneous supply.** Packs at or below about $25–40/kWh, preferably LFP, from a single source such as an OEM, bus or fleet operator, or recycler partnership. "Never-used" automotive batteries (the Fenecon route) are effectively new batteries, not second life.
2. **Minimal repurposing.** Packs reused as-is with OEM BMS read-only data (Art. 14) and string-level power electronics (the B2U model), or genuinely automated testing (Moser's claimed cost reductions are unproven at scale).
3. **A value stack that does not depend on Baltic FCR/aFRR prices.** Behind-the-meter at a grid-constrained site (EV charging hub, depot or factory), where avoided grid upgrades, peak charges and backup value carry the case. Balancing becomes an *add-on* through an aggregator (e.g., an Ignitis/Emsys-type virtual power plant), probably mFRR with its infrequent activations rather than FCR. This last point is my hypothesis, untested against Baltic activation data.
4. **Gentle operation.** A restricted SOC window (e.g., 65–15%) and modest C-rates, accepting oversizing. (Mathews)
5. **Cheap capital or grants.** EU or national grants or green financing that offset the risk premium. Steckel argues for incentives at least equal to those for first-life storage.
6. **A Europe-specific shock to new-battery prices** (tariffs, local-content rules, Chinese export restrictions) that does *not* raise used-pack prices equally. A general lithium spike is not enough, as 2022 showed.

**Bottom line for 2026–2028.** As a standalone, merchant, utility-scale balancing play in Latvia or the Baltics, second-life EV batteries most likely fail kill criteria 1, 3 and 4, and are exposed on 5–7. The case still holds in a small behind-the-meter niche with captive supply, as-is packs and grant funding, where Baltic balancing revenue is upside rather than the investment thesis.

### Gaps
